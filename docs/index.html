Money or education: what matters more for a child's success at school?

We test the hypothesis that family wealth affects a child's school results more strongly than parental education. On the interactive map you can set the living conditions of a 15-year-old student and see the average PISA 2025 maths score of children with those conditions in each country.

The three factors we compare

Parental education

The highest level of education of the two parents: basic (up to grade 9), secondary (school / college) or higher (university).

Economic capital

The family's money. We measure it by possessions at home: the number of cars, rooms with a bath or shower, and rooms with a toilet.

Cultural capital

The cultural environment at home (after P. Bourdieu): the number of books, musical instruments and works of art.

What we found (PISA 2025)

What to do about it: ideas for education policy

PISA 2025 data (636,000 students with complete data in 87 countries) show that parental education and home wealth together explain only about 9.7% (~10%) of the score differences within a country. The other 90% depend on schools, teachers, motivation, ability and chance.

The key message for education policy: a child's results are not predetermined by their background. Many children from the poorest families outperform peers from the richest ones. However, all 16 proposals below are hypotheses to pilot and evaluate, not guaranteed recipes: PISA data show precisely where the barrier arises, but cannot tell in advance which intervention will work best in a given country.

Three mechanisms the data point to

The analysis splits the family's influence into three separate channels, each of which needs its own type of tools:

Mechanism 1 · Direction A

Material conditions at home

Where basic living conditions are not guaranteed, money buys time, a quiet place to study, internet access, transport and access to a stronger school.

In the third of countries with the fewest resources, wealth matters more than education in 20 of 29 countries (coef. 0.19 vs 0.15).
Mechanism 2 · Direction B

Home learning environment

Much of the "wealth effect" is actually cultural and learning capital at home (books, a reading habit, help with homework), which school can partly compensate for.

Books at home are the strongest single factor in 47 of 82 countries (mean coef. 0.195 vs 0.110 for material possessions).
Mechanism 3 · Directions C and D

Parental education and involvement

Educated parents pass on language, academic expectations and the ability to navigate the education system. This factor dominates in most middle- and high-resource countries.

Parental education is stronger in 59 of 87 countries (+18 points per 1 SD vs +12.5 for the HOMEPOS wealth index).

Next steps (from data to action)

Step 1 · In-depth diagnosis

Comparison with reference countries

Compare the profile of a specific country (for example, Ukraine) with its neighbours in the PISA sample by wealth quintiles and vulnerable groups, or add national data (national exams, education quality monitoring).

Step 2 · Robustness check

Testing with ESCS and GDP

Check whether the findings hold using the composite index of socio-economic status (ESCS) and country income indicators (GDP per capita), which are not in the base student file.

Step 3 · Pilot design

Implementation and evaluation plan

For a chosen idea from A1–E16, develop a detailed pilot plan: costs and budget, a clear target group, success indicators and an impact evaluation protocol (RCT or comparison of similar schools) before scaling up.

How we calculated it and what the data do not tell us

Calculations

  • Data: OECD PISA 2025, 747,068 students from 89 countries and economies (complete data on parental education and wealth for 636,000 students, or 85%, in 87 countries).
  • Model on the map: a separate weighted regression for each country, score = a + b1·education + b2·economic capital + b3·cultural capital, where each factor has several levels. The regression is run for all 10 plausible values and averaged; students are weighted by W_FSTUWT. In cross-country summaries every country has equal weight, so that large countries do not "drown out" small ones.
  • Parental education is the higher level of the two parents (HISCED / PAREDINT in years of schooling: 6, 9, 12, 14, 16 years): basic = up to grade 9, secondary = school / college, higher = university.
  • Economic capital is our index of three "money" possessions at home: the number of cars (ST251Q01JA), rooms with a bath or shower (ST251Q03JA) and rooms with a toilet (ST251Q04JA). We take the mean of the standardised values and split students into quarters (quartiles) within each country.
  • Cultural capital (after P. Bourdieu) is the same kind of index built from the number of books (ST255Q01JA), musical instruments (ST251Q06JA) and works of art (ST251Q07JA) at home, also split into quarters.
  • Why two indices instead of the official HOMEPOS: HOMEPOS mixes money and culture into one number. If HOMEPOS is split into its components (82 countries with complete data), the mean standardised coefficient is 0.195 for books at home, 0.162 for parental education and 0.110 for purely material possessions.

Limitations

  • Correlation ≠ causation. The data do not prove that raising parents' income or formal education would by itself raise a child's score. Hidden factors (ability, motivation) may be passed on in the family at the same time.
  • A prediction is not the fate of a particular child, but the average score of children with these conditions in this country. Together the factors explain about 10% of the score differences within a country (up to 11–18% with extended categories).
  • Missing data are not random. Parental education is missing for 14.6% of students, and these students score on average 40 points below their country's mean (while those who answered score 7 points above it). So the most vulnerable children are under-represented, and the real gaps are probably even larger.
  • The indices are rough: both our indices and HOMEPOS rely on a 15-year-old's answers about things at home, not on the family's actual income or assets. Each of our indices has only 3 questions, so students with identical answers at quartile borders are split between neighbouring groups at random. Some countries did not ask all the questions (the Netherlands, Israel, Germany, Taiwan, QCI); there the index is built from the questions that were asked, and the map shows a note.
  • The codes QCI, QUA, QKI and QTJ refer to individual regions of countries, not whole countries. Mapping QCI → China and QUA → Ukraine is our assumption and needs to be checked against the PISA 2025 report. El Salvador and Costa Rica have no data on parental education, so only their average score is shown.
